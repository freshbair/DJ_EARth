Field notes / First machine-learning experiment

Listen to the air.

Read a NASA NO₂ map through the flute. A model trained on the older map's colour legend estimates the value at your chosen point. Higher pitch means more nitrogen dioxide in the atmospheric column. Volume stays independent.

Historical map-reading prototype. This is not today's weather, an AQI reading or a forecast. Estimates come from image colours, not the underlying satellite measurements.

Choose a map

What this model can tell us

The supervised model uses five nearest labelled colours to estimate a continuous value. It stays fixed when you change maps. The palette compatibility check does not verify dates, geography or units; confirm those yourself. Terrain shading, text and boundaries distort colours. Dark or neutral pixels are rejected, including some high-value areas. A rejected point means unknown, never zero.

Accurate temperature readings will need a temperature-specific calibration or, preferably, numerical data with coordinates, dates and units. Independent measurements are needed to evaluate scientific accuracy. Blind and low-vision listeners should help test whether the sound scale is understandable.

Source: NASA Aura OMI, northeast US, March 2015–2019 and March 2020.

Behind the sound

    One-dimensional k-means learns up to seven groups from a sample of this image, ordered from low to high across Do–Si. In “Learn from image” mode, pitches align to these learned centers.
